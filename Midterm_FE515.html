1.5 First four moments of daily log returns

You compute the four moments for each stock:

Mean: the average daily return. Variance: how spread out returns are, which is the usual measure of volatility. Skewness: whether big moves lean toward gains (positive) or losses (negative). Kurtosis: how heavy the tails are. A normal distribution has 3, so higher means more extreme days. Skewness and kurtosis are computed from the standardized central moments (population version). Kurtosis is reported as raw kurtosis (a normal distribution has 3); excess kurtosis is also shown.

moment_stats <- function(x) {
  x  <- as.numeric(x)
  m  <- mean(x)
  v  <- var(x)
  n  <- length(x)
  s  <- sqrt(sum((x - m)^2) / n)          # population sd for standardizing
  sk <- sum((x - m)^3) / n / s^3
  ku <- sum((x - m)^4) / n / s^4
  c(Mean = m, Variance = v, Skewness = sk, Kurtosis = ku, `Excess Kurtosis` = ku - 3)
}

moments_df <- data.frame(
  Stock = tickers,

Volatility. NVDA is the more volatile stock, with a daily standard deviation of about 3.24% versus about 1.42% for COST.

Skewness. NVDA’s skewness (0.23) is slightly positive, so its return distribution is nearly symmetric with a mild lean toward large gains. COST’s skewness (-0.81) is clearly negative, meaning its extreme moves are more often large losses than large gains.

Under a normal distribution, only about 0.27% of days (about 3.5 of 1,293) should fall beyond ±3σ. TSLA has 17 such days (1.31%), roughly 4.9 times the theoretical frequency. Several moves exceed 4σ and 5σ, which a normal distribution would essentially rule out. TSLA therefore shows fat tails: extreme gains and losses happen much more often than the normal model predicts.

2.5 Why extreme returns matter

Extreme returns drive most of the real losses (and gains) investors experience, and they occur more often than normal-distribution models suggest. Identifying them matters because:

  • Risk measures that assume normality (e.g., plain Gaussian VaR) can badly understate tail risk, so position sizing, stop-loss rules and capital buffers may be too small.
  • Large losses can trigger margin calls, forced liquidations and breaches of risk limits, and a single extreme day can erase months of gains.
  • Stress testing, scenario analysis, and tail-aware measures (Expected Shortfall, fat-tailed models, options hedging) rely on knowing how large and how frequent extremes are.
  • Extremes often cluster around news, earnings and market stress, which informs event risk management and diversification decisions.

3.5 Is the normal distribution a good model?

The simulated returns closely match the real data in mean (0.0022 vs. 0.0020) and variance (0.00103 vs. 0.00105), which is expected because the simulation used the sample mean and standard deviation as inputs. However, the normal model fails to reproduce the shape of the real distribution. The actual returns have kurtosis of 6.66 versus about 3 for the simulated data, and their minimum (-18.6%) and maximum (+21.8%) are far more extreme than the simulated values (-12.9% and +12.4%). The real data also show positive skewness (0.23), while the normal distribution is symmetric. The histograms confirm this: the actual returns have a higher peak and heavier tails.

Conclusion: The normal distribution is a reasonable approximation for the center of the return distribution and for the first two moments, but it is not a good model for stock returns overall. It underestimates the frequency of extreme gains and losses, so risk measures based on it (such as Gaussian VaR) would understate tail risk. A fat-tailed model such as the Student-t distribution, a GARCH model, or historical simulation would be more appropriate.
